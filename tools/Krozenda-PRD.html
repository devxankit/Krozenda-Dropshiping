1.Product overview and vision

1.1 What Krozenda is

Krozenda is a single integrated commerce platform that runs three distinct business models at the same time, sharing one product catalog, one shopping cart, one checkout, and one settlement engine. A customer browsing the platform sees a single coherent storefront; behind it, any given product may be supplied by an external dropshipping partner, sold by an independent marketplace seller, or owned outright by the platform operator.

The platform serves both retail consumers and business buyers. The same product can carry different prices for a retail customer, a dealer, a distributor and a wholesaler, with minimum order quantities enforced per tier. This dual B2C/B2B capability on shared infrastructure is the platform's central design challenge and its primary commercial differentiator.

1.2 Problem statement

Operators wanting to run dropshipping, a marketplace, and their own inventory typically end up with three disconnected systems: a dropshipping tool, a marketplace platform, and an inventory system. Stock is duplicated, orders are reconciled by hand, commission is calculated in spreadsheets, and vendor settlement is a monthly ordeal. Reporting across the three is impossible without manual consolidation.

Krozenda addresses this with a unified model in which:

  • one catalog holds products from all three sources, distinguished by an ownership attribute rather than by living in separate systems;
  • one cart can contain items from several sources and splits automatically at checkout into independently fulfilled, invoiced and settled sub-orders;
  • one commission engine resolves rates across products, vendors, categories and companies with a deterministic precedence order; and
  • one settlement engine pays every vendor through a single compliant mechanism, with a complete audit trail.

1.3 Product goals

2.The three business models

Everything in this document is shaped by the fact that three models coexist. The businessModel attribute on a product, and on the sub-order derived from it, determines who fulfils, who invoices, who is paid, and who carries liability.

Figure 2.1 — Three business models sharing one commerce spine.

2.1 Model A — Direct Dropshipping

The Admin onboards external suppliers — companies, manufacturers, wholesalers, traders, dealers and retail shops. Their products appear in the platform catalog. The customer pays into the platform, the order is automatically forwarded to the supplier, and the supplier packs and ships from its own address, uploading tracking. The platform deducts commission and settles the remainder.

AttributeBehaviour
Supplier acquisitionAdmin-initiated only in Phase 1. Supplier self-registration is deferred to Phase 2.
Catalog sourceManual entry, CSV/Excel import, or API sync from a supplier adapter.
Supplier syncCapped at two supplier integrations in Phase 1. Each further integration is bespoke, separately priced work.
FulfilmentSupplier ships from its own premises; pickup scheduled through Shiprocket against the supplier's registered pickup address.
SettlementRazorpay Route transfer to the supplier's linked account, net of commission.

2.2 Model B — Marketplace

Any seller may self-register, upload KYC documents, and wait for Admin approval. On approval the seller receives a storefront and access to the Seller Panel on both web and mobile, managing its own products, stock, pricing, orders and settlements. Fulfilment is from the seller's registered warehouse, with pickup arranged through Shiprocket.

2.3 Model C — Own Stock

The platform owner lists and sells platform-owned inventory, fulfilled from the platform's own warehouse. Inventory, orders, shipping and sales reports for own stock must be trackable separately from vendor stock — this is a reporting requirement that shapes the inventory data model, which carries an explicit owner bucket rather than inferring ownership from the product record.

Architectural requirement — do not model role as a string. Roles 8 to 11 can hold both a seller and a buyer capability on a single account. A user record therefore carries roles[] and capabilities[] arrays, never a single role field. Every authorisation check must ask "does this user hold this capability" rather than "is this user this role". Getting this wrong at the data layer forces a painful migration later, because dual-capability accounts cannot be represented at all.

3.2 Personas

Persona 1 — Platform Operations Manager (Admin)

Runs the marketplace day to day. Reviews seller KYC applications, approves products and categories, monitors orders that have stalled, handles escalated returns, configures commission, and reads the daily settlement report. Needs bulk actions, clear queues of pending work, and a reason field on every rejection so decisions are defensible later.

Persona 2 — Marketplace Seller

A small business owner listing a few hundred SKUs. Uploads products by CSV rather than one at a time, checks new orders on a phone during the day, prints packing slips in a batch each evening, and cares more about when money arrives than about anything else in the panel. Needs a settlement view that answers "when will I be paid and how much" without support.

Persona 3 — Dropshipping Partner

A wholesaler whose catalog is synced by adapter. Rarely logs in; receives forwarded orders, ships them, uploads tracking. Needs order forwarding to be reliable and settlement to be automatic. Failure mode to design against: stock drift between their system and ours causing accepted orders they cannot fulfil.

Persona 4 — Retail Customer

Buys occasionally on a phone. Cares about price, delivery date, and whether a return is possible. Will abandon checkout if it takes more than a few steps or if delivery to their PIN code turns out to be unavailable only at the last step.

Persona 5 — B2B Buyer (Dealer / Distributor)

Buys in bulk at a negotiated tier, needs a GST invoice with the correct GSTIN, and repeats similar orders regularly. Values reorder, saved addresses, MOQ visibility and clear tier pricing far above browsing or discovery features.

4.RBAC and the permission model

4.1 Principles

  • Permissions are data. Stored as records, seeded at deployment, editable by Super Admin without a code change.
  • Capability-based checks. Authorisation asks whether the user holds a named capability, not which role they are.
  • Deny by default. Absence of an explicit grant is a denial.
  • No self-escalation. No role may grant itself a permission it does not already hold.
  • Ownership scoping. Seller-surface permissions are additionally scoped to records the seller owns — a seller with order.read reads only its own sub-orders.

Figure 4.1 — Permission resolution chain.

4.2 Permission namespace

Permissions are named resource.action. The resource list follows the data model; actions are drawn from a fixed verb set so the namespace stays predictable.

7.1 Architectural principles

PrincipleConsequence for the build
Modular by domainCode is organised by business domain — auth, seller, catalog, order — not by technical layer. Each module owns its controllers, services, validators and constants.
Integrations behind interfacesNo module imports the Razorpay, Shiprocket or FCM SDK directly. They depend on PaymentProvider, LogisticsProvider, NotificationChannel and SupplierAdapter. This is what makes Phase 2 additions non-destructive and makes the whole system testable without live third-party calls.
MongoDB is the system of recordNo business state lives in a third-party service. Razorpay and Shiprocket hold their own records; ours are authoritative for our domain.
Financial values are snapshottedPrice, commission and GST rate are written onto the order at the moment of placement and never recomputed from live configuration.
Server-authoritative amountsEvery payable figure is computed server-side from the database. A price submitted by a client is never trusted.
Fail closed on moneyWhere a settlement or payout condition cannot be confirmed, the system withholds rather than releases, and raises an exception for human review.
Shared constants across the wireRole keys, status keys and business-model keys are defined once and mirrored in the frontend config, because they cross the wire as plain strings.

  • DLT dependency (client-owned): TRAI DLT entity registration, sender ID approval, and pre-registration of every template. Takes 3–10 working days and templates cannot be changed freely after approval.

Module 16 · SCOPE ADDITION — not in the SOW
Wallet and Store Credit
Refund credit · wallet ledger · redemption at checkout · regulatory constraints
This module is a scope addition (ADD). A platform wallet is not in the agreed Phase 1 scope and does not appear in the Scope of Work — "wallet" there refers only to third-party wallets as a payment method at checkout. It is specified here in full because the client requested it. It requires a written change request covering cost and timeline before any work begins, and it must not be assumed to be inside the 90-day commitment.

M16.1 Purpose

A wallet holds a balance the buyer can redeem against future purchases. It reduces refund friction — credit is instant where a gateway refund takes days — and it retains value on the platform instead of returning it to the buyer's bank.

M16.2 The regulatory constraint that shapes the design

A reloadable wallet is a Prepaid Payment Instrument. If customers can top up a wallet with fresh money, the platform is issuing a PPI, which requires RBI authorisation, an escrow account and its own compliance regime. That is a substantially larger undertaking than a commerce feature and is not proposed here.

What is specified below is a closed-loop store credit wallet: it is funded only by refunds, cancellations, goodwill credits and promotional credits issued by the platform. It is non-reloadable — a customer cannot add their own money — and non-withdrawable — the balance cannot be cashed out to a bank account. It can only be spent on the platform. This is the variant that avoids the PPI question, and the client's legal counsel should confirm the position before launch (§44 D8).

M16.5 The settlement subtlety

Wallet redemption is a discount to the platform, not to the vendor. If a buyer pays ₹1,000 of which ₹200 comes from wallet credit, only ₹800 reaches the gateway — but the vendor is still owed its full share of the ₹1,000 sale. The ₹200 must be funded by the platform from its own money. This is why wallet is not a trivial feature bolted onto checkout: it changes the arithmetic of the Route split, and the platform must top up the difference so the vendor is made whole. Any implementation that quietly short-pays the vendor by the wallet amount is a serious defect and a breach of the Vendor Agreement.

41.Acceptance criteria

41.1 Definition of done — per requirement

A functional requirement is complete when all of the following hold:

  • The behaviour is implemented and demonstrable on staging.
  • Validation and error paths behave as specified, not only the happy path.
  • Permission gating is enforced and verified for every role that should and should not have access.
  • Audit logging is present where the requirement involves a privileged action.
  • The relevant surface reflects it — an API without its UI is not done.
  • It is covered by at least one test at the appropriate level.

42.1 Financial testing — special attention

Money paths get adversarial testing rather than confirmatory testing. Specifically: attempts to release a held transfer without a delivery event; refunds issued without a matching transfer reversal; concurrent redemption or checkout on the last unit; duplicate webhook delivery; replayed webhook payloads; payout submission retried after a timeout; and reversal where the vendor balance is insufficient. Each must fail safely and leave a reconcilable record.
